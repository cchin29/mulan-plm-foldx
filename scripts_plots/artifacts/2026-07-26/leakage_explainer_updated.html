Data leakage in SKEMPI ΔΔG cross-validation

Why MuLAN's CV10 numbers overstate generalization, what the SOTA (RDE) does differently, and how far a leakage-free split has to go. Built on the real MuLAN datasets, the reproduced rng-42 fold assignment, and MuLAN's real mmseqs ≤60% sequence-identity clustering.
1 · Anatomy

Why splitting by mutation leaks

SKEMPI data is hierarchical: each protein complex contributes many single-point mutations. S1102 has 1102 mutations from just 111 complexes; S2003, 1124 from 174. When cross-validation splits the mutations at random, a complex's dozens of mutations scatter across every fold — so the same complex (same wild-type sequence, same interface, same embedding baseline) sits in both training and test. The model recalls a per-complex offset instead of predicting a fresh one, and the reported PCC measures interpolation among already-seen complexes, not generalization.

What the SOTA actually does — and a correction. RDE's SKEMPI ΔΔG cross-validation splits by complex, not by homology cluster: random.Random(2022).shuffle(complex_list) → 3 folds, with an explicit assert train∩val == ∅. The homology-clustered files in the RDE repo belong to its pretraining stage, not the ΔΔG benchmark. So a complex-based split is enough to match RDE; homology clustering (§4) is a stricter option beyond it — MuLAN's runs use mmseqs/CD-HIT ≤60% sequence identity (the iDist ≤0.03 interface dedup from PPIRef/PPIformer is a much narrower alternative).

2 · Scale

A few complexes carry most of the data

The leak bites because the distribution is extremely long-tailed. Toggle the datasets — the four MuLAN benchmarks (S1102–S4169) and the three full SKEMPI sets (combined single+multi, single-, and multi-point): in every one, a handful of protease/inhibitor complexes dominate while a large share of complexes contribute a single mutation. 2b · Scale under ≤60% clustering

Clustering collapses the complexes into a few sequence families

Same mutations — but now each tile is a ≤60% sequence-identity family (mmseqs / CD-HIT single-linkage, MuLAN's clustered split unit), not one complex. Full SKEMPI single-point's 314 complexes fold into just 93 families, and a single protease/inhibitor mega-family swallows ~40% of all mutations. Holding a whole family out is what removes the ~78–90% homolog leak the by-complex split leaves behind — but it also reveals that the number of truly independent sequence groups is far smaller than the complex count suggests. Tile area ∝ mutation count.

2c · Scale under CATH superfamily

CATH groups differently — more fragments, no single giant

Now each tile is a CATH superfamily group (both partners must share a superfamily; SIFTS pdb_chain_cath_uniprot). Counter-intuitively this is finer than the ≤60% clustering — 159 families vs 93 for single-point — because single-linkage on shared inhibitor chains merges aggressively, while CATH's both-partners rule keeps distinct antigen/antibody pairings apart. No one mega-family dominates (top three ≈35%), but antibody Ig-fold complexes (CATH 2.60.40.10) still lump together. This is the strictest, most definition-sensitive rung. 5 · Takeaways

What this means for the retrain

By-complex (matching RDE) removes the ~95% same-complex leak, but it is not the leakage-controlled number: MuLAN's own seq-id ≤60% clustering shows a by-complex test set still has a sequence homolog in training for ~78–90% of mutations — independently corroborated by USP-ddG's "88.7% easy." So the defensible, frontier-comparable number is the clustered (CD-HIT ≤60% seq-id) split, which is what MuLAN's clustered runs already report.

The measured cost is real: down the S1102 ladder the per-structure Spearman falls 0.489 (leaky) → 0.452 (by-complex) → 0.370 (clustered), and FoldX features help most precisely where the split is hardest. The CATH-superfamily hold-out is the strictest tier, and it was actually run — MuLAN was retrained and scored on USP-ddG / CATH-ddG's own CATH test set, reaching per-structure Spearman 0.411 (pooled CATH-all) against USP-ddG 0.493 / CATH-ddG 0.494, and placing 2nd on CATH AUROC (above CATH-ddG). This is the headline leakage-controlled comparison the Benchmark Matrix leads with — not an optional afterthought.
